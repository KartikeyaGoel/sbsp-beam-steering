Beam, wire, or battery?

A solar power satellite in geostationary orbit can repoint its microwave beam to a different ground station in milliseconds. Supporters call this an "interconnector in space". This page puts a number on it, using a year of real hourly prices from six electricity markets.

Run the satellite yourself

Choose a parking spot in orbit and which ground stations (rectennas) to build. Every hour the beam goes to whichever station pays most. The model runs here, in your browser, on the real hourly day-ahead prices.

Is the beam the cheapest way to get this flexibility?

Each option starts from a satellite feeding one fixed rectenna and adds one thing: a second rectenna to steer to, an HVDC line to the same second market, or a 2 GW / 8 GWh battery at the fixed site. The bars show added revenue per dollar of annualized cost. Anything above 1.0 pays for itself.

Findings

    What this leaves out

    • Energy-only, day-ahead revenue. Real-time spikes, capacity and ancillary payments would add value to every option.
    • Prices are taken as given here. A 2 GW plant would push down prices in small markets, which makes steering from a US slot more valuable and leaves the Atlantic case about unchanged (see the paper).
    • Rectenna costs are very uncertain. We scale one NASA estimate by footprint.
    • An HVDC line can also carry other generators' power. That reuse isn't credited.
    • Cross-border regulation and siting are ignored. They may matter most for the transatlantic case.
